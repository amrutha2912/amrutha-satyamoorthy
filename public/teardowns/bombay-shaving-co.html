bombay shaving company: the premium brand that went mass, and lost its moat

Men's Grooming Premium → Mass Pivot D2C + Retail Expansion
brand snapshot

india's dollar shave club moment, and the pivot that diluted it

bombay shaving company was founded in 2016 by shantanu deshpande, with a thesis you could put on a single slide: indian men deserve a better shaving experience than mass-market disposables, and the urban, image-conscious ones will pay a premium for it. the early brand was sharp. razors, shaving cream, aftershave. clean design. d2c subscription model. a clear alternative to gillette.

it worked, for a while. bsc raised over ₹200cr, built a strong d2c customer base, and became the reference point for indian d2c men's grooming. then came the pivot. body wash. face wash. beard care. hair removal cream. deodorants. skincare. the product line went from ~10 skus to 50+. distribution shifted from d2c-led to mass retail-led.

which raises the oldest question in brand strategy: can you go mass without losing the premium that got you there? bsc's trajectory suggests the answer is almost always no.

strategic evolution

the three eras of bsc

2016–2019: The Razor Era

clear positioning: premium shaving. d2c subscription model. 10 to 12 skus, every one of them shaving-adjacent. brand identity was tight. you knew exactly what bsc was the moment you saw it. comparable in the way dollar shave club or harry's were in the us. strong founder narrative on top of it: iim-l grad challenging gillette.

2019–2022: The Expansion Era

sku explosion into face care, body care, hair removal, beard grooming. ₹45cr series a, then ₹160cr. offline retail entered aggressively. positioning quietly shifted from "premium shaving" to "men's grooming." the category got wider. the brand got blurrier. it's never just a positioning shift on a slide. it's what every customer remembers about you.

2022–Present: The Identity Crisis Era

competing simultaneously against gillette (mass razors), beardo (beard and grooming), man matters (wellness), and nivea men (body care). no single category owned. the subscription model that defined early bsc isn't the primary growth driver anymore. offline retail and marketplace sales dominate. the loop was traded for the funnel.

strategic analysis

porter's five forces: why the mass pivot left bsc exposed

HIGH

competitive rivalry

bsc now competes in 5+ subcategories at once. in razors: gillette holds 80%+ market share. in beard care: beardo, ustraa. in skincare: man matters, nivea men, garnier. in body care: every fmcg brand you've ever heard of. they're up against a dominant incumbent in every single category they've entered.

HIGH

threat of substitutes

every bsc product except razors has dozens of substitutes at lower price points. a ₹200 bsc face wash competes with ₹99 nivea and ₹149 garnier. switching cost is essentially zero, because men's grooming products carry almost no lock-in. you can swap brands every shower and your life doesn't change.

MED

buyer power

men's grooming is a low-involvement category for most indian men. brand loyalty is genuinely weak, almost everyone is buying on price or availability, not preference. that gives the buyer significant power. they can switch with zero friction every time they walk into a store.

MED

supplier power

contract manufacturing in indian personal care is commoditised. bsc doesn't own its own manufacturing, they outsource. that means margins get squeezed from both directions. retail demands discounts to stock the product. contract manufacturers have limited flexibility on cost. neither side gives much.

HIGH

threat of new entrants

the d2c personal care space has almost no barriers to entry. a new men's grooming brand can launch on amazon with contract-manufactured products, instagram marketing, and ₹50l in capital. bsc's early-mover advantage has fully eroded as the space filled with competitors who didn't have to do any of the brand-building work themselves.

the five forces verdict: bsc faces high competitive pressure in every category it now operates in, with low switching costs and weak category ownership. the mass pivot turned a defensible niche (premium shaving) into an indefensible broad position (everything for men). they moved from focused differentiation into what porter would call stuck-in-the-middle. it's the worst place to be.

the one thing they're doing wrong

sku proliferation killed the brand's moat

issue tree: why is brand recall weakening?

Why is BSC losing competitive differentiation?

positioning dilution

50+ skus across 6 categories. no single category owned. "men's grooming" is a shelf label, not a positioning. a category isn't a strategy.

channel shift

d2c → retail and marketplace. subscription model effectively abandoned. no recurring revenue anchor to hold the brand together over time.

price compression

mass retail demands lower price points to stock you. premium margins erode the moment you say yes. and once you're on the shelf next to gillette and nivea, you're competing on price, not story.

the mece breakdown is clean: bsc's differentiation problem has three non-overlapping root causes, positioning dilution, channel shift, and price compression. all three trace back to the same single strategic decision to go mass. and the one most within the team's control to fix is the first one. positioning.

the brand recall test: ask 10 urban indian men, "what is bombay shaving company?" in 2018, the answer would have been "razors" or "shaving." in 2025 it's most likely "men's grooming" or "that brand i've seen on amazon." the shift from a specific association to a generic one is the cleanest signal that a moat has eroded. you can't measure it on a dashboard. but you can hear it.

the recommendation

pick a lane. re-anchor around shaving, or commit fully to the platform.

bsc has two viable paths from here. the current middle path, being everything for every man, isn't one of them.

Path A: Re-anchor on shavingPath B: Commit to grooming platform
What it meansSunset underperforming SKUs (body wash, hair removal). Rebuild subscription model around razor + cream refills. Own "premium shaving" again.Accept the broad positioning. Build a loyalty/membership tier that bundles products. Compete on convenience + bundle economics.
The betA focused brand with loyal subscribers beats a broad brand with low repeat rates.Men's grooming is a platform play, whoever owns the first purchase owns the shelf.
The riskThe premium shaving market may be too small to justify ₹200Cr+ in funding.Competing against Gillette, Nivea, and Beardo simultaneously requires massive marketing spend.
Who winsDollar Shave Club model. Harry's model. Clear precedent in the US.Dr. Squatch model. Manscaped model. Requires scale.

my recommendation is path a. the subscription razor model has proven economics globally. bsc still has the brand equity to reclaim it. and crucially, it's the path that most directly rebuilds the retention loop that they lost in the mass pivot. the loop was the moat. they gave it up. they can take it back if they're willing to give up some of the topline that came with the trade.

what i'd measure in 30 days

unaided brand recall in "premium shaving" vs "men's grooming", surveyed across 500 urban indian men aged 22 to 35.

if bsc still has stronger recall in "premium shaving" than in "men's grooming," the original positioning is recoverable. they can go back. if recall is equally weak in both, the brand has been fully commoditised in the consumer's mind, and the rebuild is much harder. this one data point determines which strategic path is actually open to them.
