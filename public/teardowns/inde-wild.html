Inde Wild: The acquisition machine that forgot to build a retention loop

How India's most aesthetically perfect beauty brand is growing revenue by finding new customers, not keeping existing ones.
Ayurvedic Beauty $12M ARR (2025) Sephora UK & US 28 employees
Brand Snapshot

The "Rhode of India", and what that comparison actually reveals

Inde Wild, founded in 2022 by influencer Diipa Büller-Khosla and husband Oleg, blends Ayurvedic heritage with Gen Z aesthetics under the coined philosophy "Ayurvedistry." In just 3 years, they've gone from launch to $12M ARR, landed in Sephora UK and US (a first for any Indian-born beauty brand), and built a digital community of 3M+ followers.

The Champi Hair Oil is their nuclear weapon, the #1 selling prestige hair oil on Nykaa, outperforming Olaplex and L'Oréal, reportedly selling one bottle per minute globally. The Dewy Lip Treatment (DLT) is their second hero SKU.

The "Rhode of India" comparison is flattering, and accurate. Like Rhode, Inde Wild converts founder social capital into product trial at a rate most brands would kill for. But the comparison also reveals the structural risk: Rhode works because Hailey Bieber's audience is effectively infinite. Inde Wild's isn't.

Growth Diagnosis

The AARRR funnel: beautiful at the top, leaking at the bottom

The probable North Star Metric, and what it should be

Inde Wild likely tracks monthly GMV or new customer acquisition rate as their primary growth indicator. Given the fundraise narrative ($1.9M → $12M ARR) and the Sephora expansion focus, the team is almost certainly optimising for top-line growth velocity.

What it should be: 90-day repurchase rate by SKU. A beauty brand's value is in the repeat, cosmetics margins are made in month 3, not month 1. If Champi Oil buyers don't come back within a usage cycle (6–8 weeks for a hair oil), no amount of Sephora shelf space fixes the economics at scale.

Why acquisition is "exceptional"

Three compounding acquisition channels working simultaneously: (1) Diipa's 3M+ personal following drives organic discovery, (2) the aesthetic packaging creates unboxing virality on Instagram and YouTube, and (3) the "Ayurvedistry" positioning occupies a unique category space that neither pure Ayurveda brands nor Western beauty brands can claim. This is unusual, most D2C brands rely on one channel. Inde Wild has three working in parallel.

Where the funnel breaks: a first-person data point

Real customer journey: I bought the Champi Oil and the DLT. The packaging is stunning, it grabs attention in exactly the right way. The product is good. But I didn't repurchase. The reason is simple: at ₹1,200+ for a hair oil, there are alternatives at half the price that do 80% of the job. The brand lingers in my brain, but not in my bathroom cabinet. This is the retention gap, beautiful enough to try, not habit-forming enough to stay.

Risk Analysis

The hero SKU concentration problem

Based on public signals (Nykaa bestseller rankings, Sephora featured products, marketing spend allocation, and founder interviews), Inde Wild's revenue is heavily concentrated in two products.

Champi Hair Oil, est. 55–60% of revenue
Dewy Lip Treatment, est. 15–20%
All other SKUs, est. 20–25%

The risk: If a competitor launches a comparable Ayurvedic hair oil at 40% lower price with comparable aesthetics (which is a matter of when, not if), Inde Wild's revenue engine is exposed. One product carrying 55%+ of revenue is a single point of failure, not a foundation for a ₹1000Cr brand.

The One Thing They're Doing Wrong

They've built an acquisition machine and called it a brand

Inde Wild's entire growth model, founder content, aesthetic packaging, Sephora distribution, PR coverage, is optimised for one thing: getting a new customer to make their first purchase. Every channel, every campaign, every product launch is about acquisition.

What's missing is the retention architecture. There is no subscription model. No repurchase trigger in the packaging. No loyalty program. No "you're running low" reminder system. No habit-building content (daily hair care rituals, weekly routines). The post-purchase experience is: buy → use → forget → maybe see another Instagram post → maybe buy again.

This model works at $12M ARR when the addressable pool of "people who follow Indian beauty influencers and shop prestige beauty" is large enough to fuel growth through new customers alone. It breaks at ₹1000Cr, where the new customer pool is no longer effectively infinite and the cost of acquiring each marginal customer rises.

The math: At an estimated AOV of ₹1,500 and a new customer acquisition cost of ₹300–500 (blended across channels), Inde Wild needs each customer to purchase at least 2–3 times to achieve healthy unit economics. If the repurchase rate is below 25%, they're spending more to acquire customers than those customers will ever return in lifetime value. The brand looks like it's growing. The economics may not support it.

The Recommendation

Build a ritual, not a product line

The Champi Oil is inspired by the Indian tradition of champi, a weekly hair oiling ritual. But the product is sold as a product, not as a ritual. That's the gap. The fix isn't a new SKU or a lower price. It's turning the single-purchase product into a recurring behaviour.

Specific actions:

1. In-packaging ritual card. A physical card inside every Champi Oil box: "Your 6-week champi ritual" with a weekly checklist. Creates a sense of journey and makes the user aware of when they'll need a refill, before they run out, not after.

2. Subscription with a first-refill hook. At week 4 of the 6-week cycle, trigger an SMS/WhatsApp message: "Your champi ritual is halfway through. Lock in your next bottle at 15% off, auto-delivered before you run out." The goal is to capture the second purchase before the customer enters comparison-shopping mode.

3. Community ritual content. Weekly "Champi Sunday" content on Instagram: real customers doing their hair oil ritual, tagged and featured. Transforms a product into a community behaviour. The social proof of seeing others do it weekly creates a pull-retention mechanic that no discount can replicate.

What I'd measure in 30 days

If I were inside Inde Wild as a growth analyst for one month, I would track exactly one thing:

90-day repurchase rate, segmented by SKU and acquisition channel.

If Champi Oil repurchase is above 30% → the retention problem is manageable; focus on increasing frequency. If below 20% → the brand has a structural problem that no amount of Sephora expansion will solve. If DLT repurchase is higher than Champi → that tells you which product has a genuine habit loop and which is a novelty purchase.
