clinikally: the activation problem hiding inside a category-creation opportunity

Skintech / Derma Beauty New Category Creation D2C + Marketplace
brand snapshot

the hardest growth problem in consumer is creating a category from scratch

clinikally sits in the gap between two extremes in indian skincare. on one side, pharmacy generics that dermatologists actually prescribe (cheap, clinical, zero brand appeal). on the other, instagram-famous beauty brands (gorgeous, aspirational, efficacy claims you'd be wise to question). their bet is that a growing segment of informed indian consumers wants both at once. clinical credibility AND brand experience.

the positioning is solid. the problem is execution at the funnel level. category creation is a special kind of growth problem. you have to educate the customer before you can convert them. but education adds steps. and every added step in the funnel is a drop-off opportunity. the more you explain, the fewer people you actually convert.

the funnel above is what almost every clinical skincare brand looks like. but the drop-off at "30+ skus" and "which formulation" is entirely avoidable. the user showed up with intent. they have a real skin concern they're trying to solve. the product experience takes that intent and converts it into confusion instead of action. that's a failure of activation, not interest.

the aha moment never arrives. for clinikally, it should be: "i used something recommended for MY specific skin concern, and it visibly worked in 2 weeks." but most users never get there. either they don't buy anything because the choice is overwhelming, or they buy something close enough but slightly wrong and don't see results. both outcomes look like activation failures, and they kill retention before it even starts.

the strategic gap: clinikally's target quadrant (high credibility, premium price) is currently held by western imports like la roche-posay and cerave. no strong indian-born brand owns this space. minimalist comes closest, but they're positioned on ingredient transparency, not clinical backing. the opportunity is real. but it's only an opportunity if activation actually converts the "interested but confused" shopper into a paying one.

the one thing they're doing wrong

they're selling a catalog. they should be selling a diagnosis.

clinikally's website and marketplace listings present products as a catalog. browse by category, compare ingredients, read descriptions. that's how a pharmacy works. it isn't how a consumer brand grows. pharmacies don't compound. brands do.

the user doesn't want to pick between 30 products. they want to be told: "based on your skin type and what you described, START HERE." that's what a dermatologist actually does in a real consultation. they don't hand you a menu. clinikally has the clinical credibility to make that exact kind of recommendation. they just aren't using it at the point of purchase, which is the one moment it would actually matter.

the recommendation

replace the catalog with: diagnosis → starter product.

what i'd actually do:

3-question skin quiz (skin type, primary concern, current routine)
→
ONE recommended starter product (not a regime)
→
One-click purchase with "dermatologist-recommended" badge
▲ Reduce activation from 8 decisions to 3

why one product, not a regime: a "complete skincare routine" (cleanser, serum, moisturiser, sunscreen) is ₹2,000 to 4,000 in one go, which creates sticker shock at exactly the wrong moment. a single starter product at ₹500 to 800 is an easy first yes. then the aha moment hits (visible improvement in 2 weeks) and the user is already pre-sold on the rest. the bundle gets sold later, after trust exists. not before.

why "dermatologist-recommended" matters: it's clinikally's only defensible moat in a market where every brand claims to be "clean" and "science-backed." the quiz output should feel like a prescription, not a product page. the wording shifts from "you might like this" to "our dermatologists recommend this for your skin type." that's the credibility premium that justifies the price premium.

what i'd measure in 30 days

quiz completion rate → first purchase conversion within 48 hours.

the quiz is the new activation gate. if quiz completion is above 60% but purchase conversion is below 10%, the recommendation at the end isn't compelling enough. if quiz completion itself is below 30%, the quiz is too long or too intimidating to start. the actual metric: of 100 people who start the quiz, how many buy something within 2 days? that single number IS the activation rate. everything else is noise.
