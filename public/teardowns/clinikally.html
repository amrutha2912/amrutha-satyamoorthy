Brand Growth Teardown · 04

Clinikally: The activation problem hiding inside a category creation opportunity

Dermatologist-backed skincare for Indian skin is the right product for the right market. But if users can't figure out which product to buy first, the funnel breaks before it begins.
Skintech / Derma Beauty New Category Creation D2C + Marketplace
Brand Snapshot

The hardest growth problem in consumer: creating a category

Clinikally occupies the gap between two extremes in Indian skincare: pharmacy generics prescribed by dermatologists (cheap, clinical, zero brand appeal) and Instagram-famous beauty brands (aesthetic, aspirational, questionable efficacy claims). Their bet is that an emerging segment of informed Indian consumers wants both, clinical credibility AND brand experience.

The positioning is strong. The problem is execution at the funnel level. Category creation requires educating the customer before converting them, and education creates friction in the funnel. The more you explain, the more decisions the customer has to make. The more decisions, the more drop-off.

The funnel above is typical for clinical skincare brands. But the drop-off at "30+ SKUs" and "which formulation" is entirely preventable. The user came with intent (they have a skin concern) but the product experience converts that intent into confusion instead of action.

The Aha Moment never arrives. For Clinikally, the Aha Moment should be: "I used a product recommended for MY specific concern and it visibly worked within 2 weeks." But most users never reach that moment because they either (a) don't buy at all due to choice paralysis, or (b) buy the wrong product and don't see results. Both outcomes are activation failures.

The strategic gap: Clinikally's target quadrant (high credibility, premium price) is occupied by Western imports (La Roche-Posay, CeraVe) but has no strong Indian-born brand. Minimalist comes closest but positions on ingredient transparency, not clinical backing. The opportunity is real, but only if activation converts the "interested but confused" shopper into a buyer.

The One Thing They're Doing Wrong

Selling a catalog when they should be selling a diagnosis

Clinikally's website and marketplace listings present products as a catalog: browse by category, compare ingredients, read descriptions. This is how a pharmacy works. It's not how a consumer brand grows.

The user doesn't want to choose between 30 products. The user wants to be told: "Based on your skin type and concern, START HERE." That's what a dermatologist does in a consultation, they don't hand you a menu. Clinikally has the clinical credibility to make that recommendation. They're just not using it at the point of purchase.

The Recommendation

Replace the catalog with a diagnosis → starter product flow

The fix in 3 steps:

3-question skin quiz (skin type, primary concern, current routine)
→
ONE recommended starter product (not a regime)
→
One-click purchase with "dermatologist-recommended" badge
▲ Reduce activation from 8 decisions to 3

Why one product, not a regime: A "complete skincare routine" recommendation (cleanser + serum + moisturizer + sunscreen) costs ₹2,000–4,000 and creates sticker shock. A single starter product at ₹500–800 is an easy first purchase. Once the Aha Moment hits (visible improvement in 2 weeks), the user is pre-sold on the rest of the regime. The bundle comes later, after trust is built, not before.

Why "dermatologist-recommended" matters: It's Clinikally's only defensible moat in a market where every brand claims "clean" and "science-backed." The quiz output should feel like a prescription, not a product page. The language shifts from "you might like this" to "our dermatologists recommend this for your skin type." That's the credibility premium that justifies the price premium.

What I'd measure in 30 days

Quiz completion rate → first purchase conversion within 48 hours.

The quiz is the new activation gate. If quiz completion is above 60% but purchase conversion is below 10%, the recommendation isn't compelling enough. If quiz completion itself is below 30%, the quiz is too long or too intimidating. The product is: if 100 people start the quiz, how many buy something within 2 days? That number IS the activation rate.
